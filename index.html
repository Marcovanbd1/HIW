The missing layer in health systems isn’t technology. It’s interpretation.

Across health and care we are investing rapidly in triage, automation, dashboards, messaging, AI documentation, and digital access. But the biggest pressure multiplier isn’t a lack of tools. It’s that people struggle to understand what to do next.

The structural problem

Most systems assume people arrive knowing what they need. In reality they arrive with uncertainty, stress, partial understanding, and cognitive overload. So navigation demand is often generated by the system itself.

Origin of this work

This approach did not begin as a product idea. It emerged from building a live neighbourhood-level digital layer designed to create shared access points and feedback surfaces across services.

Once that sensing environment existed, it became possible to see patterns of demand forming earlier and more clearly. That led to developing Neighbourhood Signals, a structured way of interpreting emerging real-world pressure.

Signals then revealed a second gap: even when systems understand what is happening, individuals still struggle to understand what to do next.

What currently exists

Built everywhere
  • Directories
  • Service finders
  • Referral pathways
  • Triage tools
Still missing

Something that:

  • interprets a person’s situation in context
  • explains what matters
  • reduces options
  • shows a clear and proportionate next step
This missing layer now has a name: Support Playlists
↑ Back to top

Support Playlists

A Support Playlist is a structured set of next steps generated from a person’s situation, not from a search query.

Two entry points. One interpretation layer.

Community entry
Someone explains what’s going on → context interpreted → Support Playlist returned

Clinical entry
Consultation or discharge ends → clinical context already known → Support Playlist generated

Why this matters now

Until recently, translating messy real-world situations into clear guidance reliably wasn’t technically feasible. Recent advances in language models now make this possible for the first time.

Where this sits in the system stack

Triage systems Decide urgency.
Directories Store services.
Booking systems Schedule care.
Clinical systems Hold records.
Support Playlists connect these layers to human understanding.
↑ Back to top

Why this works in real systems

Most digital health tools only create value when full pathway integration is complete. Support Playlists can create value immediately, even in fragmented environments. They function before transformation is finished.

Strategic positioning

Support Playlists are not:

  • a directory
  • a triage system
  • a booking tool

They are infrastructure for applied understanding.

The opportunity

The systems that scale successfully across health economies won’t be those with the most technology. They will be those that make navigation feel simple, obvious, and humane.
